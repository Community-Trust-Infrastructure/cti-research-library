# Donor-Advised Fund Deployment Incentives and Capital Stewardship

**Status:** Analytical literature note — policy proposal, not evidence of CTI effectiveness. Added 16 November 2026.

## Source

Robert Foster (6 August 2026), [“The Case for Ranking Donor-Advised Funds”](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/donor-advised-funds-ranking), *Stanford Social Innovation Review*. DOI: [10.48558/2NZM-3N46](https://doi.org/10.48558/2NZM-3N46).

**Separate contextual source:** Robert Foster's LinkedIn commentary supplied by the researcher (November 2026) on recoverable DAF investment for housing predevelopment and the “first-dollar problem.” The predevelopment example is **not** the article's central ranking proposal; its factual and quantitative claims have not been independently verified here.

## Source argument

Foster argues that US donor-advised fund (DAF) sponsors face incentives to accumulate assets rather than deploy them. He cites a reported $326 billion in US DAF assets in 2024 and proposes publishing an annual ranking for sponsors with more than $500 million in assets, using a single deployment-to-assets ratio. He argues that a transparent comparison could alter sponsor and donor incentives without mandating a payout rate.

The cited asset figure and patterns of grantmaking should be checked against the underlying DAF Research Collaborative and National Study data before being used as independent empirical findings. The proposed ranking is a normative policy intervention, not an evaluated outcome.

## CTI interpretation — distinct from Foster's claims

1. **Incentives are part of capital stewardship.** Where custody and asset-based fees reward accumulation, better intentions alone may not change allocation practices. Investigate how institutional incentives shape capital deployment.
2. **Deployment is not community accountability.** A higher deployment ratio does not establish who chose the destination, whether community knowledge was recognized, or whether funding served intended needs. CTI might support contextual, contestable evidence of allocation processes and stewardship, not a universal trustworthiness score.
3. **Reciprocal accountability.** Research should ask not only how capital providers evaluate communities, but how communities can scrutinize the commitments and decisions of capital holders.
4. **Timing and purpose matter.** The LinkedIn “first-dollar” predevelopment example concerns early-stage, recoverable risk capital. The separate CTI public sport infrastructure hypothesis concerns recurring post-construction maintenance, accessibility and programming. These are distinct financing gaps.
5. **Architectural boundary.** CTI does not manage DAF assets, set investment policy, adjudicate trustworthiness or autonomously determine eligibility. Evidence validation and decision authority must remain separate, with appropriate legal and community governance.

## Research question

Can trust infrastructure make philanthropic capital stewardship more transparent and accountable to intended communities without reducing deployment quality, legitimacy or community agency to a single performance score?

## Falsification and open questions

- Would a published deployment ratio change sponsor behaviour, or encourage superficial throughput and gaming?
- How should grants, recoverable investments, repayment and re-deployment be counted without double-counting?
- Which stakeholders should define “responsible deployment,” and how can affected communities contest those definitions?
- What disclosure is possible without exposing sensitive donor, grantee or community information?
- Which US DAF legal and tax features do not transfer to Canadian charitable vehicles or donor-retained-principal proposals?
- How should timing, loss tolerance, additionality, risk, governance rights and long-term stewardship be evaluated alongside capital movement?

## Evidence limitations

Foster's essay is an advocacy argument. The proposed metric has not been shown here to increase beneficial community outcomes. His LinkedIn example and reported donor preferences are separate, unverified claims. The US DAF context should not be treated as equivalent to Canadian donor-advised arrangements, donor-retained principal or CTI's proposed trust layer.
