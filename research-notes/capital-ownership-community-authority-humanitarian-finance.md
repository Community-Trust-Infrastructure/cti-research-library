# Capital Ownership, Community Authority and Humanitarian Finance

**Status:** Emerging comparative research hypothesis · 13 November 2026. Not a validated CTI architectural claim or assessment of any named initiative.

## Central proposition

The source of capital need not automatically determine who makes every downstream allocation decision. Financial arrangements may be able to separate capital ownership, custody, investment management, allocation mandates, community decision-making, execution and accountability while respecting legal and fiduciary constraints.

**Research question:** Can financial infrastructure preserve the economic and legal rights of capital providers while enabling affected communities to exercise meaningful, enforceable authority over decisions about resources intended for their benefit?

## CTI boundary

CTI does **not** prescribe which actor should have authority, invest or custody assets, select beneficiaries or operate settlement rails. It may help make evidence, authorization provenance, delegation scope, validation, revocation and accountability independently inspectable. Governance determines legitimate authority; CTI helps convey the evidence and context needed to evaluate it; financial systems execute authorized decisions.

A useful test: **Can community-authorized decision makers make an allocation the capital provider would not independently have selected, and will the system honour that decision within an agreed mandate?** If not, participation may be advisory rather than binding. This is a proposed diagnostic, not a universal definition of community legitimacy.

## Comparative cases and open questions

### FASE Foundation — humanitarian finance (case for investigation)

Primary announcement: [Tether and collaborators launch FASE Foundation](https://tether.io/news/tether-and-industry-collaborators-launch-fase-foundation-to-fight-scams-support-trafficking-survivors-and-pilot-stablecoin-aid-in-vulnerable-communities/) (Tether, 2026).

Investigate who sets priorities and eligibility, controls custody and disbursement, validates community claims, has appeal rights, and can revoke or contest decisions. The announcement is a primary-party account, **not independent evidence** of community authority, safeguards, implementation outcomes or effectiveness. Examine its emergence in the changing U.S. humanitarian-assistance environment without assuming a causal relationship to USAID restructuring.

### Donor-retained capital — philanthropic finance (hypothesis)

Compare models in which donors retain principal while communities exercise defined authority over **realized distributable returns**, subject to enforceable mandates, tax law, investment risk, fees and liquidity constraints. See [donor-retained capital research note](donor-retained-capital-community-directed-yield.md). Do not infer Canadian charitable-receipting eligibility or durable yields.

### SportFi — implementation research environment

Investigate the distinction between community voice, legitimate delegation and technically enforceable allocation rules. SportFi does not establish that the donor-retained capital or FASE governance models work.

## Proposed authority map

| Function | Candidate responsibility / authority |
| --- | --- |
| Capital ownership and withdrawal rights | Donor, foundation or legal asset owner |
| Investment and custody | Authorized financial institutions or managers |
| Purpose, restrictions and allocation mandate | Legally agreed governance arrangement |
| Allocation decisions | Community-authorized decision makers, where delegation is genuine |
| Evidence and validation | Context-appropriate, authorized attestors and validators |
| Execution and settlement | Separate financial infrastructure |
| Accountability, correction and recourse | Defined oversight, appeal and revocation processes |

## Research agenda

1. Document actual legal and operational control, not only participatory language.
2. Identify whether authority is advisory, delegated, binding, revocable and contestable.
3. Compare FASE and other humanitarian mechanisms with donor-advised funds, endowments, ordinary donations of investment income and community-controlled funding.
4. Examine privacy, sanctions/AML, donor restrictions, safeguarding, tax eligibility, financial sustainability and risks of shifting liability to communities.
5. Evaluate whether systems honour legitimate decisions that funders would not independently select.
6. Separate claims made in organizational announcements from independently verified findings.

**Interpretive caution:** Programmable payments can execute rules; they cannot by themselves establish whether the rules or their authorizers are legitimate. No endorsement or partnership is implied by including a case.
