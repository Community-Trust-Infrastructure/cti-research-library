# Donor-Retained Capital and Community-Directed Yield

**Status:** Exploratory research — not a validated CTI implementation.

## Research question

Can capital ownership, investment returns and community decision-making be separated in ways that preserve donor participation while increasing community agency?

## Hypothesis

Donors may be able to retain ownership of invested principal while making realized, distributable returns available for community-directed funding.

Community Trust Infrastructure could provide evidence, validation and governance context for accountable allocation decisions without managing investments, holding donor assets or determining investment strategies.

## Research priorities

- Compare donor-retained capital with conventional charitable endowments, donor-advised funds and investment-income donations.
- Investigate Canadian charitable receipting and legal requirements.
- Examine governance models for community-directed distributions.
- Evaluate investment risk, liquidity, costs and sustainability.
- Explore potential implementations using existing financial infrastructure.

## Public sport infrastructure stewardship — exploratory application

Infrastructure investment creates long-term stewardship obligations that are frequently separated from the original capital funding decision.

This research explores whether donor-retained capital and community-directed investment returns could help finance the ongoing maintenance, accessibility and programming of public sport infrastructure, while preserving municipal oversight and increasing transparency and community participation.

This is a proposed application, not an established financing model. Further investigation is needed into municipal responsibilities and liability, investment and liquidity risks, eligible uses of distributions, public accountability, and community decision-making authority.

## Structural funding hypothesis — November 2026

**Research objective:** The objective is not to make nonprofits better at competing for scarce operational funding. It is to investigate whether financial infrastructure can reduce the need for that competition altogether.

**Primary research question:** Can financial infrastructure enable recurring, community-directed funding from existing capital, reducing nonprofit dependence on competitive grants while preserving local decision-making authority?

Donor-retained principal with community-directed distributions is one candidate mechanism, not a required architecture. Other possible comparators include endowments, municipal investment income, cooperative finance and public–philanthropic arrangements. The research distinguishes capital availability from legal accessibility, distributable returns from headline yields, and community authority from donor or institutional control.

**Evaluation dimensions:** (1) Predictability and continuity of net distributable funding; (2) change in fundraising and administrative workload, including new verification costs; (3) enforceable local decision-making authority; (4) additionality versus displacement of existing charitable gifts; (5) investment, liquidity, currency, legal and governance risks.

**Infrastructure stewardship application:** Initial construction grants may leave municipalities with long-term safety, maintenance, accessibility and programming obligations. Investigate whether supplementary, committed distributions can support these costs without displacing municipal oversight or assuming variable returns can guarantee essential safety services. Compare with an upfront municipally controlled lifecycle reserve.

**Architecture boundary:** Investment custody, strategy, yield generation and financial settlement sit outside CTI. CTI may support evidence, attestations, validation, accountable allocation and community governance. Bread Cooperative and SportFi remain exploratory reference cases, not validation or commitments by partners.

**Open tests:** Can multi-year commitments, withdrawal conditions and buffers make distributions sufficiently dependable? Does the mechanism reduce competition for grants or merely relocate competition? Are communities able to make binding allocation decisions? Can costs and risks be measured against conventional funding?

## Potential reference cases

Bread Cooperative and SportFi offer relevant mechanisms and implementation experience for investigation. Neither should be treated as proof of the proposed donor-retained capital model.

## Invitation

We welcome research, legal perspectives, implementation examples and critical analysis that could help establish whether this model is feasible, beneficial and appropriate for community finance.
